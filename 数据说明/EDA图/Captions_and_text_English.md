# Section 4. Exploratory Data Analysis (captions and draft text)

Except for Figure 6(a), all statistics use training-set samples only (2025-09-30 to 2026-06-12, 232,608 samples). A sample is a minute with at least one trade that is not within 3 days before a contract roll: in those days the expiring contract is being abandoned, so its liquidity and spread do not represent the contract one would actually trade, and these minutes (4.83% of the training set) get no labels. Isolated no-trade minutes within a session are filled as flat bars and used only to complete look-back windows, never as samples (see Sections 2 and 5). Labels use the default setting: k_SL = 2, reward-to-risk 1:1. Figures are produced by `scripts/plot_eda_figures.py`; numbers are in `eda_stats.txt`.

## 4.1 Target distribution

As Figure 1 shows, in the training set stop-loss is the most common class for all 6 output heads (39.2%–44.7%), take-profit is 33.3%–38.3%, and timeout is stable at 22.0%–22.5%. The reason is the bid-ask spread: a long trade enters at the ask and is judged against the bid (the reverse for shorts), so the take-profit line is farther away and the stop-loss line is closer. The gap narrows for longer horizons, because the barrier distance scales with √H while the spread stays fixed. No class is rare, so resampling is not needed, but stop-loss exceeds take-profit by 1–11 percentage points; we can use class weights and evaluate with macro-F1 or log loss rather than accuracy alone. The validation and test sets have the same structure (stop-loss largest, gap narrowing with horizon), but more stop-losses (40.2%–47.3%) and fewer timeouts (18.8%–21.2%); see "non-stationarity" in Section 7. The model's probabilities therefore need recalibration on the validation set.

> **Figure 1. Label distribution of the 6 output heads: training, validation and test sets.** Stop-loss is the largest class for every head in all three sets; timeout is about 22% in training and about 19%–21% in validation and test. The stop-loss excess comes from the spread and shrinks at longer horizons. **Implication for modeling:** the imbalance is moderate, so no resampling is needed; use frequency-based class weights and macro-averaged metrics; validation and test have more stop-losses, so probabilities should be calibrated on the validation set.

## 4.2 Input distributions

Figure 2 shows 4 key input features on a log-count scale. The close change d_close (this minute's close minus the previous close, divided by ATR14) is close to normal (kurtosis 2.7; 1st/99th percentiles about ±1.7), so standardization is enough. The other three have long tails: ofi_depth_adj still has kurtosis 239 after the time-of-day adjustment; spread/ATR14 has a median of 0.18, a 99th percentile of 0.55 and kurtosis 496, mostly from the daily reopen, holidays and the expiring contract just outside the 3-day pre-roll window (before that window was excluded, kurtosis was 2,680 and the 99th percentile 4.25); relative volume relvol_60 has kurtosis 35,088, driven by a few values in the first minute after a roll (new-contract volume divided by the thin average volume of the old contract, up to 472). Those samples have look-back windows that cross the roll and are dropped anyway. Such extremes would dominate the gradients, so we winsorize at the training-set 1st and 99th percentiles; spread_atr and relvol_60 are positive and can also be log-transformed before standardization.

> **Figure 2. Distributions of 4 key input features (training set; log y-axis; dashed = 1st and 99th percentiles).** The close change is near-normal; order-flow imbalance, spread and relative volume are heavy-tailed (kurtosis 239–35,088). **Implication for modeling:** standardize the ATR-normalized price-change features (d_open, d_high, d_low, d_close); winsorize heavy-tailed features at training-set percentiles, or log-transform them first. Thresholds must come from the training set only.

## 4.3 Group structure

In this project a "group" is a trading day (CME trading day, opening 18:00 ET). Figure 3(a) shows 174 trading days with samples in the training set, about 1,380 samples per normal day (one per minute), and fewer on early-close holidays and on days partly inside the 3-day pre-roll window. Samples from the same day share market conditions, and neighboring samples have heavily overlapping input windows and holding periods. Figure 3(b) measures this: two samples 1 minute apart have the same long-30-minute label 85.1% of the time, versus 35.5% for random pairs; agreement falls to the random level only once the gap reaches the holding period (15, 30, 60 minutes). A random split would therefore put near-identical samples in both training and test sets and overstate performance. We split by time and drop 60 minutes (the longest holding period) at each boundary, which is the basis for the split in Section 3.

> **Figure 3. Group structure.** (a) Samples per trading day; (b) share of sample pairs k minutes apart with the same label (dotted vertical lines = holding periods). Labels are strongly correlated for gaps shorter than the holding period and reach the random level after it. **Implication for modeling:** no random splitting; split by time with a gap at least as long as the longest holding period (60 minutes), and use time-blocked cross-validation.

## 4.4 Metadata vs. target

Figure 4(a) shows the long-30-minute label mix by US Eastern hour. The timeout share ranges from 14.5% (19:00) to 29.6% (18:00, right after the daily reopen), and is also high at 10–12 and 16 ET; at 8–9 ET (US data releases and the open) timeouts are fewest and stop-losses most common (48.2% at 9 ET). Time of day is therefore related to the label, and time features (tod_sin, tod_cos and session dummies) may help. Figure 4(b) shows the same shares by month: stop-loss rises from 40.7% (Oct 2025) to 47.1% (Jun 2026) and timeout falls from 24.4% to 19.6%, so the data are not stationary. Class shares in the validation and test sets may differ from training, and calibration should be checked on the validation set.

> **Figure 4. Metadata vs. target (training set, long 30 min).** (a) Timeout share varies between 14.5% and 29.6% across US Eastern hours; (b) by month, the stop-loss share drifts up and the timeout share down. **Implication for modeling:** time features may be useful but may also act as a shortcut (Section 4.6); class shares drift over time, so predicted probabilities need recalibration on the validation set and results should be reported by month.

## 4.5 Random examples per class

Figure 5 shows 4 random training samples from each class (seed 42, extreme-spread minutes excluded): the per-minute bid high-low for 30 minutes after entry, the entry price (ask), and the take-profit and stop-loss lines; a star marks the first minute a line is touched. All 12 agree with their labels: take-profit samples touch the take-profit line first, stop-loss samples touch the stop-loss line first, and timeout samples touch neither within 30 minutes. This figure complements the label audit in Section 2 (60 further samples, 360 labels, 0 mismatches).

> **Figure 5. 4 random training samples per class (long 30 min).** Panel times are US Eastern. Grey bars = per-minute bid high-low; black line = entry (ask); blue/orange dashed = take-profit/stop-loss; star = first touch. **Implication for modeling:** the labeling rule is applied correctly; barriers are evaluated on bid/ask rather than mid prices, so the model learns outcomes net of the spread.

## 4.6 Shortcut checks

We checked 4 possible shortcuts.

1. **Market direction.** Figure 6(a) shows the weekly long take-profit rate minus the short take-profit rate, together with the GC close. The weekly difference is strongly correlated with that week's gold price change: the correlation is +0.76 (train), +0.73 (validation) and +0.85 (test) over 34 / 7 / 7 full weeks (weeks defined in US Eastern time). In rising weeks longs hit take-profit more often, in falling weeks (e.g. March 2026) shorts do. This holds in all three splits, so the labels largely follow market direction. The risk is a short-term momentum rule ("it has been rising, so go long") that fails at reversals. **M4 check:** compare against a simple baseline that predicts from the sign of the past-60-minute price change (ret_60), and report long and short performance by month and by market direction.
2. **Flat lines and quiet periods.** We expected quiet periods, with little price movement, to be almost all timeouts. Figure 6(b) does not support this: across relative-volume deciles, the quietest decile has the lowest timeout share (19.4%), with the rest at 21.6%–23.8%. The barrier distance scales with ATR, so it is also smaller in quiet periods. Timeouts depend mainly on time of day (Figure 4a), so time features are the more likely shortcut. **M4 check:** retrain without time features and compare performance on the timeout class.
3. **Contract rolls.** In the 3 days before a roll the expiring contract is very illiquid: the median spread is 20 ticks (4 normally), most extreme-spread (≥ 20 ticks) minutes fall in these days, and almost all of them were stop-losses (79.6% before exclusion, versus 43.5% for normal minutes). A model could easily recognize them from the spread feature and learn a shortcut that holds only for the expiring contract; in live trading one would trade the new contract on those days. We therefore extend the rule "no label if the holding period crosses a roll" to "no label if minute t is within 3 days before a roll" (4.83% of training, 6.09% of validation; no roll falls in the test period). After exclusion, extreme spreads are only 0.29% of training samples (2.36% before), and their stop-loss share is 46.5%, close to 43.4% for normal minutes, so this shortcut has largely disappeared. Figure 6(c) shows both: before exclusion (top) extreme-spread and normal minutes have very different label mixes; after exclusion (bottom) they are close. Prices are proportionally back-adjusted to remove roll gaps. **M4 check:** still report normal- and extreme-spread samples separately, and do not trade when the spread is extreme.
4. **Time-of-day features.** See point 2 and Figure 4(a).

> **Figure 6. Shortcut checks.** (a) Weekly long minus short take-profit rate (bars; blue = longs better, orange = shorts better) and GC close (black line); numbers at the bottom are, per split, the correlation between the weekly difference and the weekly gold price change. (b) Timeout share by relative-volume decile. (c) Label mix for extreme-spread (≥ 20 ticks) vs. normal minutes: top, before excluding the 3 pre-roll days (extreme-spread stop-loss 79.6%); bottom, after (46.5%, vs. 43.4% for normal minutes). **Implication for modeling:** the weekly direction effect follows the market (correlation above +0.7 in all three splits), so the main risk is a short-term momentum shortcut, to be tested with a simple baseline and per-period evaluation; quiet periods do not imply timeouts; after excluding the 3 pre-roll days, extreme-spread samples have a label mix close to normal minutes, so the earlier "extreme spread → stop-loss" shortcut came mainly from the days before rolls.

**Note:** Figure 6(a) uses validation and test labels, but only to look at class shares per split; no features, models or hyperparameters were chosen from them.

**Summary of shortcut checks (long / short 30 min, training samples):**
- **Contract roll:** we checked the label mix of extreme-spread minutes (≥ 20 ticks); before exclusion 79.6% were stop-losses (43.5% for normal minutes), and most fell in the 3 days before a roll. Action: no labels in the 3 pre-roll days; afterwards extreme-spread samples are only 0.29% of the training set and their stop-loss share is 46.5%, close to 43.4% for normal minutes, so the shortcut is essentially gone.
- **Reopen hour:** we checked the label mix in the first hour after the 18:00 ET reopen; the timeout share is 29.6% (long) / 30.0% (short) versus 22.1% in other hours, and spreads are widest (Fig. 8). This is a real pattern that repeats every day, not a data defect, so time features stay; results will be reported by hour, and M4 may skip opening trades in that hour.
- **Market direction:** we checked the correlation between the weekly long-minus-short take-profit rate and the weekly gold price change: +0.76, +0.73 and +0.85 in train, validation and test, so the weekly difference follows the market. Action: M4 compares the model with a simple baseline using only the past-60-minute direction and reports results by month and by market direction.
- **Degraded-quality days:** we checked the label mix on these 5 days (3,342 training samples): long take-profit / stop-loss / timeout 38.5% / 42.7% / 18.8% versus 34.1% / 43.4% / 22.5% on other days. With about 110 effective samples (one per 30 minutes) the standard error is about 4–5 pp, so the difference is within noise; `flag_degraded_day` is not a model input, so the model cannot exploit it. Action: keep these days; M4 runs one sensitivity check retraining without them.

## 4.7 How long volatility persists (evidence for the look-back length L)

Figure 7 shows the autocorrelation of 1-minute relative changes in training samples for lags 1–240 minutes. The relative change is r = ln(close / previous close), computed only when two bars of the same contract are exactly 1 minute apart; changes across halts, missing minutes, rolls and the 3 pre-roll days are excluded. Figure 7(a): price direction barely persists — a rise this minute does not make a rise next minute more likely; the lag-1 autocorrelation is −0.011 and no lag exceeds 0.013 in absolute value. 80 lags fall slightly outside the ±0.004 band, but that band assumes independent, equal-variance observations and is too narrow for data with volatility clustering, and correlations of 0.01 have no practical predictive value. Figure 7(b): the autocorrelation of |r| decays slowly, still 0.25 at lag 60 and 0.18 at lag 240. This mainly reflects different volatility levels across trading days (e.g. the turbulent market from late January to early February 2026), not a single burst lasting 240 minutes. After removing the time-of-day pattern and each day's volatility level, the autocorrelation falls from 0.17 to half that value by lag 16 and to a quarter by lag 55; it is 0.044 at lag 60 and 0.029 at lag 120. On the scale the model actually sees, |r| divided by the previous minute's ATR14, it is close to zero after lag 6.

> **Figure 7. Autocorrelation of 1-minute relative changes (training samples).** (a) Price direction barely persists; (b) raw |r| (blue, nothing removed) seems to persist for a long time, but mostly because turbulent days are turbulent all day; the orange line removes the time-of-day pattern and the daily level, showing that a burst of above-normal volatility fades by three quarters within about 60 minutes; after dividing by ATR14 the persistence disappears within minutes. **Implication for modeling:** a 60-minute window covers a volatility burst from onset to most of its decay. Longer-term volatility levels cannot be captured by extending the window to 120 minutes and are already summarized by features such as atr_rel, vol_ratio_5_60 and ma_dist_240. L = 60 is therefore the default, and M4 compares 30, 60 and 120 minutes on the validation set as the final evidence.

## 4.8 Time-of-day pattern in spread and volatility

All times in this section are US Eastern; "Sunday" means the session from the Sunday 18:00 open to midnight (the CME trading week starts on Sunday evening). Figure 8 shows, by weekday × ET hour, the median per-minute average spread and the median 1-minute range (high − low) of training samples; cells with fewer than 100 minutes are left blank.

**Spread (Fig. 8a):** widest right after the daily 18:00 reopen, with medians of 5.8–6.8 ticks (widest on Sunday evening), then narrowing hour by hour to about 4 ticks by 23:00. All other hours are 3.2–4.6 ticks, narrowest in the US afternoon (12–15 ET).

**Volatility (Fig. 8b):** largest at 9–10 ET, with a median 1-minute range of $3.3–3.8 (US data releases at 8:30 and the COMEX day-session open); next at 8 and 11 ET ($2.6–3.2); $2.2–2.5 at 20–21 ET (Asian morning open); smallest at 16 ET before the close ($1.0–1.3). The Sunday-evening reopen is also volatile ($2.6–3.1).

**Weekday matters far less than hour:** within the same hour, Monday to Friday look alike; Sunday looks different mainly because it only contains the first hours after the reopen.

> **Figure 8. Spread and volatility by weekday × US Eastern hour (training samples).** (a) Median per-minute average spread (ticks; 1 tick = $0.10); (b) median 1-minute range (USD/oz). Sunday row = session after the Sunday 18:00 open; blank = daily halt (17:00) or fewer than 100 minutes. **Implication for modeling:** trading cost relative to price movement varies strongly by hour: at 16 ET the spread is about one third of the 1-minute range, at 9–10 ET only about one ninth. Because the take-profit and stop-loss distances scale with ATR, the spread takes the largest share of that distance in quiet hours and at the reopen, so time of day affects the label (consistent with Fig. 4a). Time features (tod_sin, tod_cos, session dummies) can represent this pattern; weekday effects are small, so the weekday dummies are expected to matter little. Results will be reported by hour, and M4 may consider not opening trades in the first hour after the 18:00 reopen.

**Note:** Figure 8 uses training samples only (traded minutes outside the 3 pre-roll days), so it differs slightly from Figures 3–5 in Section 2, which use all training-set minutes. The heatmap previously shown in Section 2 has moved here.
